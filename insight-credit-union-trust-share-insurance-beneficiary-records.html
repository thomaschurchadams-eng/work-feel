Trust Account Insurance Changes December 1. Start With the Beneficiary Records

November 13, 2026

By CreditUnionAI News Staff

Risk and Compliance

NCUA’s December 1 change combines revocable and irrevocable trust coverage. Check repeated beneficiaries and missing records before discussing limits.

Two trust accounts can look separate on a credit union’s system and still count together for federal share insurance. Starting December 1, 2026, the National Credit Union Administration will combine covered revocable and irrevocable trust funds into one insurance category. For deposit operations and compliance teams, the work starts with the grantor and the beneficiary records—not with a per-account coverage quote.

Count people, not account numbers

Under the 2024 final rule, trust funds passing from the same grantor to beneficiaries at the same federally insured credit union are generally combined. The calculation allows up to $250,000 for each eligible, distinct beneficiary, counting no more than five per grantor. The new trust category remains separate from funds properly insured in a different ownership category. The effective date is for the trust-account change; it is not the date on which every provision in the final rule takes effect.

Consider a simplified example. One grantor has a payable-on-death account naming two eligible beneficiaries and a qualifying formal trust account naming those same two people plus a third. Under the new formula, the ceiling for the combined trust funds in that example is $750,000, not $1.25 million. Five beneficiary entries are not five unique beneficiaries. The example assumes the funds and beneficiaries qualify and is not an insurance determination.

Use the right records for the right accounts

For an informal revocable trust, such as a payable-on-death account, the final rule requires beneficiaries to be named in the credit union’s account records. Formal arrangements are different. The NCUA may need to obtain beneficiary information from the trustee or other competent sources; the rule describes a presumption for eligible beneficiaries of formal revocable trusts specifically named in the credit union’s records. A blank field on a formal trust screen does not settle the question.

Run a sample for each account type. Start with cases where the same grantor appears on different products, where an informal account lacks a usable beneficiary record, or where a share certificate matures after December 1. Compare records under the institution’s existing privacy and access procedures. Give frontline staff a clear place to refer exceptions instead of having them resolve ambiguous trusts at the counter.

Update the script before the tool

The NCUA’s fact sheet explains the new calculation and identifies circumstances that may affect coverage. Its public estimator still describes its older calculation and warns about the December change. Before giving a member an estimate, check which rule the tool actually applies. Label the date and assumptions, and escalate incomplete or unusual cases. A worksheet is a conversation aid, not a guarantee of how the NCUA will determine an insurance claim.
