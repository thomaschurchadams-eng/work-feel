Eighth Circuit Vacates Fed’s Debit Interchange Fee Cap

October 27, 2026

By CreditUnionAI News Staff

Regulation IIDebit InterchangePaymentsRevenue Planning

The court affirmed that the Federal Reserve used impermissible costs in setting its debit-fee standard. A replacement rate is not set, but the planning assumptions beneath debit programs have changed.

The U.S. Court of Appeals for the Eighth Circuit has affirmed a decision vacating the Federal Reserve’s 21-cent debit interchange cap and sent Regulation II back for a rewrite. The October 26 decision does not itself publish a replacement fee or direct a network to change pricing today. It does, however, move a long-running legal risk into a concrete planning event for credit-union payments, finance and fraud teams.

In Corner Post, Inc. v. Board of Governors, the panel said the Durbin Amendment requires the Fed to base the standard on the marginal cost of a particular debit transaction. The court held that the agency improperly allowed fixed or non-transaction-specific costs into the calculation. It affirmed the district court’s judgment vacating the cap and remanded the matter to the Fed.

The current standard for covered issuers is 21 cents plus 0.05% of the transaction value, with a potential one-cent fraud-prevention adjustment. The Fed proposed a lower formula in 2023, but the appeals court said that unfinished rulemaking did not resolve the legality of the existing rule.

The ruling narrows which costs can support a cap

The distinction is operationally important. Regulation II’s existing formula included costs associated with authorization, clearing and settlement, network processing, fraud monitoring and fraud losses. The court concluded that fixed costs—such as a one-time software investment that does not change with transaction volume—cannot be counted merely because they support debit processing.

The panel did not require each fee to equal the exact marginal cost of its transaction. It said the Fed may use a reasonable proxy, potentially including average variable cost, if the result remains reasonably related to marginal processing costs. That leaves material design work for the agency and uncertainty around the eventual formula, implementation date and any further judicial review.

The district court had stayed its vacatur pending appeal. Credit unions should not infer from the appellate opinion alone that the familiar cap has already disappeared from network settlement files. The Fed or other parties may seek rehearing, a further stay or Supreme Court review, and the appellate mandate and agency response still matter.

The small-issuer exemption remains, but economics can travel

Regulation II exempts issuers with less than $10 billion in assets, together with affiliates, from the interchange fee standard. That statutory boundary did not change in this opinion. Most credit unions therefore are not directly subject to the capped formula.

Exemption is not the same as insulation. Network pricing, merchant routing behavior, processor contracts and competitive account economics can transmit a lower covered-issuer benchmark across the market. Larger credit unions at or above the threshold face the direct rate-setting risk; smaller institutions need to test whether their networks and program partners preserve distinct exempt-issuer economics.

The ruling also separates fraud costs from the court’s permitted cost base in a way that can widen the management gap between safety investment and recoverable interchange revenue. Finance and fraud leaders should make that tradeoff visible instead of treating interchange as a stable offset to card losses, disputes and monitoring.

Build a decision-ready debit scenario now

A practical response begins with three scenarios rather than a single forecast: current economics continue during review; a new formula materially reduces the covered benchmark; or network and routing changes narrow exempt-issuer yields even without a direct statutory cap. Each scenario should connect gross interchange, network and processor expense, fraud loss, dispute operations, rewards, free-checking economics and member fees.

Payments teams should confirm the institution’s exemption status and affiliate treatment, then document how the processor and each enabled network classify exempt transactions. Treasury or finance should identify which product assumptions fail first under lower net revenue. Legal and compliance should monitor the appellate mandate, any rehearing or Supreme Court petition, and the Fed’s formal response before approving member-facing changes.

No institution needs to preemptively reduce fraud controls or reprice an account because of one opinion. The immediate decision is whether leaders can produce a reconciled debit-program view quickly enough to evaluate a future Fed rule or network response. A documented trigger—such as a final mandate, stay decision, proposed replacement standard or contract notice—keeps scenario work separate from premature execution.
