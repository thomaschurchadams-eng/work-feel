UK Regulators Sketch How Critical-Provider Oversight Could Be Funded

November 24, 2026

By CreditUnionAI News Staff

Vendor Management

A UK discussion paper asks who should fund oversight of systemic technology providers—and what evidence a future fee model should use.

The Bank of England, Prudential Regulation Authority and Financial Conduct Authority are asking how they should recover the cost of overseeing critical third parties that provide systemic services to UK financial firms and market infrastructure.

The November 24 discussion paper covers charging principles, the tariff base, the fee model and the method for calculating fees. Those are questions for consultation, not a final tariff or a new charge already in force.

The requested income data is part of the design question

The regulators are also requesting systemic third-party-services income data from designated critical third parties and other providers that may consider themselves likely candidates for designation. That information could affect how a future tariff base allocates oversight costs across providers.

Responses are due January 19, 2027. The regulators say they expect to provide another update in Spring 2027, which may include a consultation on proposals after they consider the feedback. The paper does not set final fee amounts, a final formula or a completed provider-by-provider allocation.

What credit-union vendor teams can take from it

The UK framework is not a U.S. credit-union requirement. Its practical value is the governance question it makes visible: when a shared technology provider becomes systemically important, institutions still need a clear record of which services are critical, what revenue or usage measure supports cost allocation, and how regulatory or resilience costs may flow through contracts.

The Bank’s current critical-third-party overview says the regime focuses on risks to UK financial stability from disruption and does not replace firms’ own outsourcing and operational-resilience responsibilities. That distinction matters: direct oversight of a provider does not transfer a financial institution’s due-diligence, contingency-planning or concentration-risk obligations.

For a credit union, the useful questions are whether contracts identify the exact critical service, disclose fee pass-throughs, preserve audit and incident evidence, separate shared-platform costs from institution-specific work, and provide workable exit and recovery paths. The discussion paper signals where UK oversight may go; it is not evidence of a settled cost model or a U.S. mandate.
