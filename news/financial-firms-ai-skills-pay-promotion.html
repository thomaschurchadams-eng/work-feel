Financial Firms Start Tying AI Skills to Pay and Promotion

October 14, 2026

By CreditUnionAI News Staff

WorkforceAI SkillsPerformance ManagementGovernance

A survey of 500 finance executives found AI proficiency moving into formal reviews and compensation decisions even though firms do not yet have a settled formula.

AI proficiency is beginning to affect raises, bonuses and promotion at financial firms, shifting the issue from optional training to a consequential employment control. A survey reported by Reuters on October 14 found that nearly one in four firms ties at least one of those outcomes to employees' AI skills, while 30% require workers to disclose AI use in formal performance reviews.

The survey, conducted by financial-services recruitment firm Selby Jennings, covered 500 finance executives. It found that 42% of respondents had introduced formal criteria for evaluating AI skills and 30% had created teams or roles to measure whether AI investment was producing results. The firm also told Reuters that no established formula yet determines how much compensation should be tied to AI use or performance.

That limitation matters. The sample covers the finance industry broadly, not credit unions specifically, and the Reuters report does not publish response rates, question wording or institution-size splits. The findings identify a workforce-management direction; they do not establish a sector benchmark or prove that AI-linked incentives improve performance.

Measure job outcomes, not visible tool use

A credit union that rewards the number of prompts, tools opened or AI-generated drafts will encourage activity that may not produce better service. A stronger scorecard starts with the job: resolution quality and repeat contacts in member service; documentation completeness and exception handling in operations; cycle time, error correction and control adherence in back-office work.

AI use can then be treated as an explanatory input. A review record should show which approved tools were used, the work they supported, the human checks applied and the measured result. Employees should not receive credit for concealed automation, but disclosure alone is not a business outcome.

Access and opportunity need their own evidence

Before AI skill becomes a rating factor, leaders should document whether each affected employee had comparable access to approved tools, training time, practice assignments and technical support. A score can otherwise measure organizational opportunity rather than employee capability.

The Equal Employment Opportunity Commission advises employers to communicate performance standards, apply them consistently and support evaluations with factual detail. The agency has also warned that software used to monitor performance or determine pay and promotion can disadvantage people with disabilities without appropriate safeguards and accommodation processes.

Those principles point to a practical control: compare rating, pay and promotion outcomes across roles, locations, managers and relevant employee groups, then investigate meaningful differences before decisions are final. That review should be performed by accountable human leaders, not delegated to the same system whose impact is under examination.

Separate proficiency from automated evaluation

Evaluating an employee's ability to use AI is not the same as allowing AI to evaluate the employee. The first can rely on work samples, documented judgment and observed control discipline. The second may introduce opaque scoring, monitoring and data-quality risks into a consequential employment decision.

The voluntary NIST AI Risk Management Framework emphasizes defined roles, accountability and measurement across the AI lifecycle. For a credit-union review process, that translates into named owners for skill definitions, approved evidence, accommodation and appeal paths, outcome monitoring and authority to pause the program if results become unreliable or inequitable.

A staged approach is safer than immediately changing pay. Start with voluntary skills inventories and role-specific learning goals. Validate a small set of work measures. Run a shadow evaluation that does not affect compensation. Compare manager consistency and employee access. Only then should leaders decide whether any demonstrated skill belongs in a formal review—and explain the standard before it takes effect.
