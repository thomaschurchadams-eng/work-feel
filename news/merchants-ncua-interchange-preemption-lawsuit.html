Merchants Sue NCUA Over Federal Credit Union Interchange Rule

November 16, 2026

By CreditUnionAI News Staff

Risk and Compliance

A November 12 complaint challenges NCUA’s interchange rule in Washington. The filing is separate from the Illinois appeal and does not change the law.

A coalition of merchant trade groups sued the National Credit Union Administration on November 12 in federal court in Washington, D.C., challenging the interim rule that the agency says preempts Illinois’ restriction on certain card interchange fees for federal credit unions. The filed complaint is a new challenge to the agency’s action, separate from the Illinois appeal over the state law. The filing itself does not change what an issuer must do.

Illinois’ law targets interchange fees on the sales-tax and gratuity portions of card transactions. The dispute is not about whether a payment network can identify those portions today, nor is it about the separate federal debit interchange cap. Who ultimately has to apply the Illinois rule is a question for the courts; how to apply it if required is a separate question for issuer and processor teams.

Why the court matters

In its June announcement, the NCUA said federal credit unions’ authority covers interchange and other fees even when set by or in consultation with third parties. It said the interim rule was intended to displace state laws affecting those charges. The merchant plaintiffs say the Federal Credit Union Act did not give the agency authority for this rule, and they challenge how it was issued, including the absence of notice and comment before issuance. They seek to set the rule aside and prevent its enforcement. Those are the plaintiffs’ positions and requests, not a judge’s findings.

In September, the district court in Illinois extended its injunction against applying the state law to federal credit unions after considering the NCUA rule. That decision is part of the separate Seventh Circuit appeal. The group also filed a separate challenge to the national-bank regulator’s actions, the National Association of Convenience Stores confirmed. An order in any one of these cases needs to be read for which regulator, rule and issuers it actually covers.

Keep the legal trigger and the processor plan separate

For a federally chartered issuer, the practical response to the filing is to track court orders and identify who decides whether they affect the credit union’s program. That is not a reason to change interchange logic now. The rule cited in the complaint addresses federal credit unions. A state-chartered institution should not assume that federal insurance puts it under the same ruling or filing.

Card operations can ask the processor what tax and gratuity data it would need to apply an exclusion, which fields its merchants and networks can provide, and how a split amount would show up in settlement and exceptions. Document any missing fields and the time required to test a change. Keeping that readiness work separate from the legal decision makes it possible to act on a relevant order without treating a complaint as an operating instruction.
