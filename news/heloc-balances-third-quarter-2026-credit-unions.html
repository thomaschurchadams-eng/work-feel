HELOC Balances Post Their Largest Quarterly Rise Since 2005

November 12, 2026

By CreditUnionAI News Staff

Lending and Collections

Home equity borrowing expanded again in the third quarter. Credit union lenders should separate that signal from a mortgage reporting correction and from mixed delinquency trends.

Home equity line of credit balances rose by $23 billion in the third quarter of 2026, reaching $481 billion, according to the New York Fed’s November 10 household debt release. It was the largest quarterly increase since 2005. HELOC credit limits also expanded, by $31 billion.

For credit union lending teams building next year’s production plans, those figures provide a reason to examine demand for home equity credit. They do not establish how much of the growth came from credit unions, which borrowers drew on existing lines, or how the money was spent.

The most eye-catching housing number requires particular care. Mortgage balances increased by $257 billion, but the full quarterly report says roughly half of that increase reflected balances returning to the data after a reporting gap in the previous quarter. Treating the entire increase as new borrowing would overstate the change in household behavior.

Growth and credit quality tell different stories

The report marks an eighteenth consecutive quarterly increase in HELOC balances. Across all household borrowing, however, the share of debt in some stage of delinquency reached 5.0%, up from 4.7% in the previous quarter. Those totals combine products with different performance patterns.

The New York Fed’s reported flow of balances into serious delinquency was 1.17% for HELOCs, compared with 1.27% a year earlier. The comparable mortgage figure increased from 1.28% to 1.60%. These measures track balances becoming at least 90 days late; they are not the percentage of all balances already in serious delinquency. The release describes HELOC serious-delinquency transitions as flat from the preceding quarter.

That combination matters for portfolio interpretation. Rising outstanding balances and a relatively stable transition rate can coexist. Neither measure, taken alone, tells a lender whether a new group of borrowers is performing better or whether an existing group is using more of its available credit.

A national benchmark needs a local explanation

A useful internal comparison would separate new lines, increased limits and additional draws on existing accounts. A credit union whose balances are growing mainly through repeat draws faces a different planning question from one adding new borrowers. The first needs to understand utilization and repayment capacity; the second needs to understand acquisition costs and the performance of recent originations.

These are analytical questions prompted by the release, rather than findings about credit unions. The public report draws on anonymized Equifax credit records across lenders. It does not identify a credit union segment or provide a ready-made forecast for an individual institution.

The New York Fed’s methodology notes add two useful cautions for finance and risk presentations. Balances are nominal, without an inflation adjustment. The credit-score charts also switched to VantageScore 4.0 in the first quarter of 2026, so comparisons across that boundary can reflect a model change as well as a change in borrowers.

For a lending committee, the strongest use of this release is a product-level comparison with the credit union’s own experience. The HELOC expansion is a clear national development. Its effect on local opportunity, concentration and repayment risk still needs to be established from member-level portfolio data.
