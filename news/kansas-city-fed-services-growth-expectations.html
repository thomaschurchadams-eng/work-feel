Kansas City Fed Finds Slower Services Growth, Stronger Expectations

November 22, 2026

By CreditUnionAI News Staff

Lending and Collections

November’s activity index eased while firms’ outlook improved. Credit-union lenders should keep current borrower cash flow separate from survey expectations.

Tenth District services activity continued to expand more slowly in November, while expectations improved, the Federal Reserve Bank of Kansas City reported on November 20. The divergence is useful context for credit unions serving local businesses, not a substitute for borrower-level evidence.

The November survey puts the monthly composite index at 3, down from 10 in October. The revenue/sales index fell from 16 to 2, and employment moved from 2 to minus 4. Expectations for future activity rose from 4 to 20.

These are diffusion indexes, not percentage growth rates. A reading of 3 does not mean regional service revenue grew 3%, and an expectations reading of 20 is not a forecast of 20% growth.

Keep the comparison on the revised series

The bank’s release says November incorporates new seasonal-adjustment factors, so historical indexes differ slightly from earlier releases. Comparisons should use the current series rather than mix an old October figure with the newly adjusted November result.

The survey covers varied services businesses across the district: Kansas, Colorado, Nebraska, Oklahoma and Wyoming, northern New Mexico and western Missouri. It is not a survey of all U.S. businesses or of credit-union commercial borrowers.

For lending teams, firmer expectations can inform a conversation about planned inventory, hiring or working capital. They do not verify that a particular borrower has realized those sales. A hypothetical borrower expecting a stronger season still needs a repayment assessment grounded in its own records and assumptions.

The practical reading is therefore two-part: current growth cooled, while the outlook improved. Keeping those statements separate avoids turning business optimism into current cash flow—or interpreting a slower positive index as proof that every firm is contracting.
