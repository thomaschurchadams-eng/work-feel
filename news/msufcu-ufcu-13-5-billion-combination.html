MSUFCU and UFCU Plan $13.5 Billion Combination

October 14, 2026

By CreditUnionAI News Staff

ConsolidationStrategyMember ExperienceTechnology Integration

The boards approved a deal that would create an 850,000-member institution operating several brands across Texas, Michigan and Illinois.

MSU Federal Credit Union and University Federal Credit Union have approved a definitive agreement to combine, putting a proposed $13.5 billion, 850,000-member institution on a path toward regulatory review and a UFCU member vote. The deal is consequential now because it fixes the intended charter, corporate name and leadership structure while leaving the hardest member-facing work—approval, integration sequencing and measured service outcomes—ahead.

The two credit unions announced the agreement October 13. MSUFCU has more than $9.3 billion in assets, over 415,000 members, 38 branches and more than 1,400 employees. Austin-based UFCU reports $4.2 billion in assets, more than 430,000 members, 30 branches and over 800 employees.

Upon completion, the organization would use University Federal Credit Union as its corporate name and MSUFCU's federal charter. April Clobes, MSUFCU's current president and CEO, would become CEO; UFCU President and CEO Michael Crowl would become president. The institutions expect legal completion in summer 2027, but the proposal remains subject to regulatory approval and approval by UFCU members.

Until then, the organizations say they will operate independently. Members are not being asked to change account numbers, cards, loan agreements or digital-banking access. The MSUFCU brand is expected to remain in Michigan and Illinois, and UFCU in Texas. The combined company would also retain several other brand names already operated by MSUFCU.

Legal Day One is not systems conversion

That separation matters. A legal merger can precede the migration of deposits, loans, cards, payment files, online banking, fraud controls, contact centers and data systems. Leaders should publish a dependency map that distinguishes legal ownership, operational control and the date each member-facing capability actually moves.

The credit unions say formal integration planning will progress after regulatory approvals. That sequencing protects the approval process, but it also means the announcement does not establish a core-system choice, conversion calendar, branch consolidation plan or cost target. It reports no expected expense savings, revenue synergies or member benefit measures. Those claims should remain prospective until the institutions disclose a plan and results.

Multi-brand continuity needs one control map

Keeping local brands can reduce member disruption, but it can also hide operational complexity. The combined institution would need a clear record of which legal entity, charter, privacy notice, insurance disclosure, routing path, complaint channel and service standard sits behind each brand and digital experience.

A useful integration scorecard would track digital availability, call wait times, unresolved complaints, payment exceptions, fraud losses, access changes and member attrition by legacy institution and channel. Aggregate results could obscure a problem concentrated in one geography, product or conversion cohort.

The announcement says every employee will have a position in the combined organization. That is a concrete continuity commitment, not proof that roles, decision rights and workflows will stay unchanged. Management should identify accountable owners for each integration domain, publish escalation paths and document where duplicate authority ends after Legal Day One.

Member approval needs decision-grade evidence

UFCU members will be voting on more than scale. Materials should distinguish binding terms from management intent, explain the charter and governance structure, describe any executive compensation or retention arrangements required in the formal notice, and show how members can compare present service with the proposed operating model.

Independent reporting from WKAR on October 13 confirmed the stated asset and member totals and noted that the resulting institution would rank among the nation's 20 largest credit unions. Scale makes the proposal newsworthy; it does not establish that members will receive better prices, technology or service.

For other credit unions, the operating lesson is to treat legal close, brand continuity, systems conversion and member outcomes as separate gates. Our coverage of the Sharetec outage and failover risk shows why critical-service dependencies need explicit fallback plans. The product outcome gate offers a durable way to connect promised investment to measures that can later support a continue, change or stop decision.
