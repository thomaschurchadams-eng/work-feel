A UK Crisis Exercise Shows What Recovery Rehearsals Need to Prove

November 24, 2026

By CreditUnionAI News Staff

OperationsRisk and Compliance

A three-week UK insurance exercise tested decisions under evolving information. Its useful lesson for credit unions is how to rehearse recovery—not a new U.S. requirement.

The Bank of England says a three-week general-insurance crisis exercise showed the value of putting recovery decisions under time pressure while information, assumptions and dependencies kept changing.

The November 23 release concerns the UK’s Dynamic General Insurance Stress Test, or DyGIST. It is not a U.S. credit-union requirement and did not test credit unions. The Bank says 25 insurers representing more than 80% of the UK general-insurance market participated in May 2026.

The exercise tested decisions, not a single pass-or-fail result

The published results say firms received a changing scenario and submitted financial and operational information during the exercise. The Bank describes the work as exploratory and says it was neither a comprehensive resilience assessment nor a pass-or-fail test of individual firms.

That distinction matters. A rehearsal can reveal where a response depends on optimistic assumptions without proving that every real event will be contained. The useful evidence is narrower: who made each decision, what information was available, which dependency was assumed to work, and whether the recovery action remained executable as conditions changed.

What a credit-union rehearsal can borrow

Make assumptions visible. Record whether liquidity, vendor service, specialist staffing, communications and member-service capacity are confirmed, contractually available or merely expected. A shared provider may face simultaneous demand from many institutions.

Change the facts during the exercise. A static tabletop can reward a memorized plan. Introduce new losses, unavailable staff, delayed vendor recovery, conflicting public information or a manual-workaround bottleneck, then require leaders to document how priorities change.

Separate response from recovery. Containment is not the same as restoring complete and reliable service. Track which member functions return, which remain degraded, how backlogs are reconciled and what evidence is needed before the institution declares recovery complete.

Credit unions should fit that approach to their own scale, risk profile and U.S. supervisory obligations. The UK exercise offers a rehearsal method and a set of questions—not a finding that a particular U.S. institution or service is resilient.
