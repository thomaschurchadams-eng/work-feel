Illinois Appeals Ruling Shielding Federal Credit Unions From Swipe-Fee Law

October 15, 2026

By CreditUnionAI News Staff

PaymentsInterchangeLitigationCompliance

The state appealed a permanent injunction that added federal credit unions to the institutions protected from Illinois' interchange-fee limit.

Illinois has appealed a federal ruling that prevents the state from applying its interchange-fee limit to federal credit unions, reopening the legal path while leaving card issuers with a July 2027 implementation date and a charter-specific compliance problem. The practical consequence is not an immediate reversal of the injunction. It is that payments, finance and legal teams still need a documented plan for what applies today, what could change on appeal and which institutions remain exposed.

America's Credit Unions reported October 14 that Illinois Attorney General Kwame Raoul filed a notice of appeal after the district court barred enforcement of the Illinois Interchange Fee Prohibition Act's fee limit against federal credit unions. The plaintiffs, including America's Credit Unions and the Illinois Credit Union League, filed a cross-appeal, and the case was transmitted to the Seventh Circuit.

The law would prohibit interchange fees on the tax and gratuity portions of covered electronic-payment transactions in Illinois. Its start date has been delayed to July 1, 2027. That runway reduces immediate conversion pressure, but it does not eliminate the need to preserve transaction-level data, processor requirements and revenue scenarios while the appeal proceeds.

The September order is the current control point

On September 22, the U.S. District Court for the Northern District of Illinois granted a permanent injunction preventing Illinois from enforcing the fee limit against federal credit unions, national banks, federal savings associations, certain out-of-state state-chartered banks and payment card networks. The available appeal notice does not report a stay or dissolution of that order.

The court's federal-credit-union analysis relied on an NCUA interim final rule that took effect June 30. The NCUA rule says federal credit unions may receive non-interest charges and fees, including interchange fees set through third-party relationships, and that state laws limiting those charges are preempted.

The appeal therefore reaches beyond one calculation field. It tests whether the NCUA's federal preemption position can continue to shield federal credit unions from the Illinois fee restriction. The plaintiffs' cross-appeal also keeps other parts of the case in play, including the position of institutions that do not share the same federal-charter protection.

State-chartered institutions need a separate lane

The September injunction did not create one answer for every credit union. America's Credit Unions said it remains focused on protecting state-chartered credit unions. That distinction should appear explicitly in implementation inventories, legal analyses and processor conversations rather than being buried under a generic “credit union” label.

A multi-charter organization or service provider should map each issuing entity, the member accounts and cards attached to it, the applicable network and processor configuration, and the state-law assumption used in budgeting. A shared platform can still require different legal and operational treatment across institutions.

Credit unions should also separate the fee limitation from the law's data-use restriction. The district court said its earlier treatment of the data-use provision remains in place. Conflating those two provisions could produce an incorrect implementation decision even when the institution understands its status under the fee injunction.

Keep the implementation work reversible

Card operations should preserve the capability to identify tax and gratuity amounts, reconcile processor outputs and test exception handling without assuming that every planned production change will be required. Finance teams can maintain at least two revenue and cost scenarios: the current-injunction baseline and a scenario in which the Illinois limit applies to more transactions or entities after further litigation.

Vendor-management teams should require processors and networks to state which transaction fields they can pass, what configuration changes would be needed, how testing would work and how quickly a prior setting could be restored. Legal teams should attach each operational assumption to a dated court order, rule or counsel decision so later changes do not rely on institutional memory.

The broader lesson is to govern this as a moving legal dependency, not a one-time compliance project.
