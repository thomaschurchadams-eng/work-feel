CUES Survey Flags Five-Year Credit Union CEO Succession Gap

October 26, 2026

By CreditUnionAI News Staff

Succession PlanningExecutive CompensationTalent DevelopmentBoard Governance

Roughly one-third of surveyed credit union CEOs expect to retire within five years while people, digital and cybersecurity roles gain prominence.

Nearly one in three credit union CEOs surveyed by CUES expect to retire within five years, putting leadership continuity on a timetable that boards can no longer treat as an annual paperwork exercise. The same survey signals that succession work must extend beyond the chief executive: chief people officer roles are expanding, chief marketing and experience positions are becoming more common, and digital-transformation and cybersecurity jobs are gaining prominence.

The October 22 CUES compensation survey release draws on responses from more than 300 credit unions. It reports that executive compensation rose about 4% year over year and says retention, health benefits and long-term leadership continuity remain prominent concerns.

The release does not publish the full sample design, asset distribution or role-by-role compensation tables. Its figures are therefore a directional industry benchmark, not a compensation recommendation for any individual institution. The operating value is the combination of a visible retirement horizon and a broader set of roles that need credible internal or external pipelines.

A successor list is not readiness evidence

A board can name potential successors and still be unprepared for a transition. A usable record should distinguish emergency coverage from long-term succession, identify the decision authority for each, and show what evidence would make a candidate ready.

For each critical role, the record can include current role scope, named interim coverage, candidate pool, required experiences, completed development assignments, unresolved gaps, retention risk and the next board review date. The objective is not to rank employees with a black-box score. It is to give directors a traceable view of readiness and expose where the institution is relying on one person or an untested assumption.

Because executive roles are changing, the position description also needs review. A future CEO may need different digital, partnership or risk-governance experience than the incumbent. Likewise, a growing people, experience or cybersecurity function may require a succession path before the role becomes vacant.

Compensation benchmarks need institutional context

A reported 4% average increase does not settle what a particular credit union should pay. Boards and compensation committees should compare role scope, asset size, geography, performance, internal equity and the reliability of the peer group before using a survey result. Any adjustment should remain a documented governance decision rather than an automatic response to a headline percentage.

The same discipline applies to retention. If a role is difficult to replace, the institution should identify the operating dependency, development alternative and retention objective before selecting a compensation or benefits tool. That keeps the decision tied to continuity risk instead of rewarding scarcity without a plan to reduce it.

Build one quarterly succession evidence review

A practical quarterly review can cover CEO continuity, the highest-risk specialized roles and the development moves due before the next meeting. The board should see changes since the last review: candidates added or removed, completed assignments, readiness gaps that narrowed, roles with no credible coverage and any change in expected retirement timing.

Management should also test the emergency path. That means confirming interim authority, regulator and staff communications, account access, signing limits, vendor contacts and decision records. The long-term path can then focus on selection criteria, candidate development and transition milestones without being confused with the first days of an unexpected vacancy.
