What the Proposed Vendor-Risk Guidance Would Change for Credit Unions

November 12, 2026

By CreditUnionAI News Staff

Vendor Management

NCUA and the banking agencies want oversight to reflect the risk of each vendor relationship. The useful question is how to justify that judgment when a credit union lacks complete information or bargaining power.

Credit unions have until November 16 to comment on a proposal that would put more emphasis on the risk of an individual vendor relationship and on the judgment used to accept what remains unresolved. NCUA’s deadline notice confirms that the proposed guidance is nonbinding and applies to the work banks and credit unions do with third parties.

The practical issue is familiar: a small institution may depend heavily on a provider while having little ability to negotiate its contract or obtain every requested document. The proposal addresses that imbalance directly. It also raises a difficult question about when flexibility improves oversight and when it leaves important risks insufficiently examined.

This remains a proposal. The agencies announced it on September 11, and the Federal Register published it on September 15. The approaching comment deadline should not be confused with an implementation date.

Proportionality would become the organizing principle

The OCC’s summary describes an approach based on the magnitude and likelihood of harm, adjusted for an institution’s size, complexity and risk profile. Its stated aim is to avoid processes that treat every vendor as inherently high risk. The guidance would not itself create enforceable standards.

The proposed text expressly includes insured credit unions. It separates risk identification, proportionate oversight, residual-risk acceptance and governance. It recognizes that an institution may lack the bargaining power to obtain preferred contract provisions, extensive due diligence or detailed monitoring.

For a vendor manager, the resulting decision is more demanding than simply shortening a questionnaire. Suppose two providers give incomplete answers about recovery arrangements. One supports a limited administrative task; the other is essential to member access. A useful assessment would explain the different consequences of failure and the alternatives available, rather than count missing answers as though the relationships were equivalent.

That example is an analytical application of the proposal, not an agency-approved classification. Its value is in making the judgment reviewable. A board or risk committee needs to understand the exposure it is accepting, even when the available information is imperfect.

Residual risk still needs an owner

The draft says agencies do not expect elimination of all third-party risk. It allows for reasonable acceptance where further mitigation is impractical, disproportionate or constrained by limited alternatives. It also preserves the institution’s responsibility for safe and sound operations and compliance with applicable law.

This creates a useful distinction between a known limitation and an overlooked one. A team might accept a standard contract after assessing the consequences of a provider outage and the institution’s available response. That is a different decision from renewing because no one has evaluated the dependency.

A practical management record would state the missing information, the consequence that matters, who accepted the remaining exposure and what development would cause reconsideration. Those elements are this publication’s suggested way to make a decision visible; they are not a new regulatory form or a prescriptive requirement in the proposal.

The distinction also matters for internal reporting. A document-completion percentage can describe progress through a review. It cannot, by itself, show whether the credit union could continue serving members after a major provider failure. An assessment earns its place in a board packet by connecting the vendor relationship to that operating consequence.

The consumer-compliance question remains contested

The proposed text says additional consumer-compliance considerations are not directly addressed. Federal Reserve Governor Michael Barr’s dissent argues that the proposals could leave supervisory gaps or create confusion if existing guidance is rescinded. He also questions whether emphasis on material financial risk could delay correction of problems. Those are his stated objections, rather than demonstrated effects of a final policy.

The agencies’ joint announcement also distinguishes this interagency proposal from a separate proposed guide for Federal Reserve-supervised traditional community banks. The latter should not be treated as a credit union instruction merely because it was released alongside NCUA’s joint proposal.

For a credit union considering a comment, a concrete example may be more useful than general support for less paperwork. Explain which information a provider will not supply, how that limits an actual risk decision and what clarification would help. The eventual guidance will be more useful if it makes reasonable judgment easier to defend while keeping the consequences for members visible.
