AI Trust, Intended Use and Member Outcomes Are Different Measures

November 23, 2026

By CreditUnionAI News Staff

Member Service

A dated PwC survey measures confidence and expectations—not credit-union adoption or completed member service. Keep those evidence types separate.

A consumer can trust an AI tool, expect to use one next year and still need a person to resolve today’s problem. Those are different observations. Credit-union member-service leaders should keep them separate when turning a financial-services survey into a product or staffing decision.

What the survey measures

PwC’s survey published November 16, 2026 reports that 51% of consumers trust AI-powered financial tools during market volatility, compared with 53% in June. Separately, 88% of financial-services executives say technology and AI are becoming more important for helping clients navigate uncertainty. These are different questions answered by different populations—not two sides of an adoption rate.

The same study reports that 85% of consumers value human financial guidance even as AI advances. Its October 8–15 fieldwork covered 1,000 U.S. consumers and 202 financial-services executives. The public methodology does not identify a credit-union-only sample. Nor does the two-percentage-point trust difference, by itself, establish a statistically meaningful decline.

Intention is not a completed task

A statement about future reliance describes an expectation. A trust answer describes an attitude. Neither proves that a member used the credit union’s tool, received a correct answer or completed the intended action. Even an observed login is not evidence that a dispute was opened or a payment problem resolved.

For a proposal built around an outside survey, ask the sponsor to state the decision it can support. A broad attitude finding may justify local research. It cannot, without additional evidence, establish how many employees to remove from a service queue or what share of members will delegate financial actions to an assistant.

Keep the outcome evidence separate

The CFPB’s June 6, 2023 research report on chatbots describes risks including inaccurate answers, difficulty with complex problems and obstructed access to human help. It is historical research, not a new 2026 rule or a finding about the credit union’s current system. It supplies a useful challenge to a survey-led business case: what happens after the conversation begins?

Build three separate evidence columns in that case: what members say, what they actually use, and what the service completes. For each, name the population, observation period, task and limitation. Do not combine a national survey denominator with a local usage numerator. Keep complaints and failed handoffs visible alongside successful interactions.

A practical local comparison could examine one defined service task across existing channels under the institution’s normal privacy and access controls. Retain the question asked, whether a qualified owner received it, repeat-contact rate and final disposition. Label an illustrative test as a test; do not report it as a measured improvement before results exist.

The Velera payments-trust data brief shows another reason to distinguish frequent AI use from willingness to authorize financial transactions. The decision here comes earlier: choose the evidence that answers the decision, rather than stretching an attractive percentage into a claim it cannot support.
