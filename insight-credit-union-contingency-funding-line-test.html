Can Your Credit Union Draw on Its Emergency Funding Line?

November 13, 2026

By CreditUnionAI News Staff

Finance and Treasury

A test draw can reveal what a borrowing agreement cannot: who can act, what collateral is available, and when funds actually settle.

A credit union can have a signed borrowing agreement and still be unable to move money when it needs it. The difference might be a signer who is away, collateral that is already pledged, or a missed settlement cutoff. A contingency funding exercise should test whether treasury can get usable funds to the right account—not just whether someone can reach the lender by phone.

Know what the rule requires

NCUA’s liquidity rule applies to federally insured credit unions. Those under $50 million in assets need a basic board-approved written framework and a list of sources they can use under adverse conditions. At $50 million or more, the rule requires a documented contingency funding plan. At $250 million or more, it also requires documented access to at least one contingent federal liquidity source and advance planning and periodic testing. The rule recognizes the Federal Reserve Discount Window and qualifying Central Liquidity Facility membership arrangements as ways to establish federal access.

The 2023 interagency addendum recommends regularly testing contingency borrowing lines and planning for the practical difficulty of moving collateral during stress. That is longstanding guidance, not a new rule or a guarantee that any particular line will fund.

Run the drill against a clock

1. Get to an authorized request. Give the team a realistic deadline and assume its usual signer is unavailable. Have the designated backup find the current borrowing instructions, verify their authority, and contact the counterparty through a known channel. Record any handoff that depends on one person.

2. Show what can be pledged. Reconcile collateral eligible under the counterparty’s terms against current valuations, liens and existing pledges. Confirm what the lender already holds and what the credit union would have to move. The Federal Reserve’s readiness guidance highlights borrowing agreements, pre-pledged collateral and periodic transactions. An approved line is not the same as the amount available to draw today.

3. Prove settlement and the backup path. Where internal approvals and counterparty terms permit a test transaction, capture the request time, confirmed amount, settlement time, destination and reconciliation entry. Where only a tabletop is approved, label the settlement time an estimate. Then remove the first source from the scenario and check whether the backup has independent collateral, an available signer and a workable cutoff.

Give ALCO evidence it can act on

Bring the asset-liability committee a short record: how much was usable, when it could arrive, which steps were observed versus assumed, and who owns each fix. If staff were able to request funds but could not settle or reconcile them, report that distinction. The useful result is a tested borrowing path—or a specific reason it cannot yet be relied on.
